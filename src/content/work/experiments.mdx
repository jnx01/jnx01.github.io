## Context

These three self-directed studies form one research program under a single
theme: **making AI systems efficient and reliable enough to deploy in the
real world.** Each is a reproducible repo with a README, a report, and an
honest limitations section. One of them surfaces a fragile, nuanced finding
rather than a clean win — and I'm prouder of it, not less, because it reports
the result exactly as it came out.

## 1. Budgeted model routing — *efficiency*

**Question.** If we can only afford the big, expensive model on some
questions, can a simple rule pick which ones — and beat random selection?

**Method.** Two models (Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B). Seventy practice questions
calibrate which subjects benefit most from the larger model; then 140 held-out
test questions compare three policies: always-small, random allocation
(averaged over 500 lotteries), and category-gain routing that sends high-gain
subjects to the big model.

**Finding.** Smart routing beat random at **every** budget tested. At a 25%
budget the router reached 33.6% accuracy against random's 30.7%; at 50% it
captured most of the big model's benefit (36.4% vs 33.5%). The sweet spot was
a 50% budget — most of the value, half the cost.

<RoutingCurve />

**Limitations.** One model family; only five practice questions per subject,
so single questions swing estimates by ~20 points; subject-level routing is
crude.

## 2. RAG corruption — *reliability*

**Question.** How does a RAG system's answer quality degrade as supporting
evidence is progressively removed — or replaced with misleading documents?

**Method.** Eighty hard HotpotQA questions, each starting with ten paragraphs
(two supporting, eight distractors). Four conditions: clean, one support
removed, both supports removed, and contaminated (both removed plus two
misleading distractors added). BM25 retrieves the top four paragraphs;
GPT-OSS-120B generates answers.

**Finding.** Degradation is severe and steady. Answer F1 falls from **0.649**
(clean) to **0.440** with just one support removed — a ~33% drop — and to
**0.337** with both gone. Contamination is slightly worse than absence
(0.314). Encouragingly, the model rarely hallucinated: it degraded toward
caution, not invention.

<CorruptionBars />

**Limitations.** Eighty questions is trend-level, not definitive; one model,
one dataset, and simple BM25 retrieval rather than a modern stack.

## 3. Budgeted evaluation — *a real signal, with a twist*

**Question.** Under a fixed evaluation budget, does targeting tasks where
cheap evaluators disagree beat random allocation?

**Method.** One hundred SummEval comparison tasks. Two cheap "scout"
evaluators (GPT-3.5) and one strong evaluator (Gemini Pro). Three spending
policies — scouts-only, random targeting, and disagreement-aware targeting —
replayed 500 times with 95% confidence intervals.

**Finding.** Disagreement is a real signal — when the two scouts came from
the same model family, the cases they disagreed on had nearly double the
error (57.1% vs 30.1%), and targeting them beat random allocation. The twist:
the expensive evaluator turned out to be **worse** than the cheap scouts
(52.0% vs 32.0% error), so the best strategy in this setup was to spend
nothing. And in a follow-up with a different model pair, the disagreement
signal vanished entirely.

**The lesson.** A signal is only as good as what it routes to — and it's
fragile across model pairs. This is the kind of nuanced, not-very-clean
finding that doesn't get published enough, and it's why I run these
experiments in the open.

## What ties them together

All three ask the same underlying question from different angles: *how do we
get reliable behavior from AI systems without unlimited budget?* The routing
study attacks **efficiency** — getting useful behavior for less cost. The
other two attack **reliability** — the corruption study probes robustness to
degraded evidence, and the evaluation study probes whether we can even
*measure* that behavior trustworthily.
